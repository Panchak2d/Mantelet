# Background: the problem and why this project exists

This page explains the problem Mantelet studies, what the evidence says about its size, and what the evidence cannot tell us. It is written for parents, teachers, journalists and researchers. You do not need technical knowledge.

## The problem

Some computer programs take an ordinary photo of a real person and make a fake nude or sexual image of that person. The picture is fake. The harm to the person is not.

These programs are easy to find and use. A report by the Institute for Strategic Dialogue (ISD) identified 181 websites that offer tools to make nude images of real people. It estimates those sites had more than 40.1 million unique visitors a month on average from December 2025 to March 2026. The figures come from third-party traffic estimates and cover only sites found on the open web. ISD says the true scale is likely larger. Of 163 of these sites that ISD checked for age controls, only 2 had any measure to verify that users were over 18. ISD also reports that these tools are typically built on open-source image models that anyone can download and run on their own computer.

The attack needs one photo. The photo can come from a social media profile, a school website or a group chat.

This page names no such tool and links to none.

## What the evidence says about size

No single number tells us how many people are affected. Each figure below measures something different. Read what it measures before you compare it with another.

| Figure | What it measures | Source and limits |
|---|---|---|
| 2.2 percent of respondents reported being victims of deepfake pornography. 1.8 percent reported perpetration behaviours. | Self-reports from an online survey of more than 16,000 adults in 10 countries | Academic study (Umbach, Henry and Beard, CHI 2024). Online panel, adults only, survey run in 2023. Self-report cannot be checked |
| About 1 in 8 young people aged 13 to 20 said they know someone who was targeted by a deepfake nude. 2 percent said they had made one. | A US survey of 1,200 young people | Thorn, March 2025. Thorn works on child safety and designed the study. We found no independent check. The press release gives no margin of error |
| 15 percent of high school students said they know about an AI-made deepfake sexual image of someone linked to their school. | Awareness of such images in US public schools in the 2023 to 2024 school year | Center for Democracy and Technology survey. It measures awareness, not the number of victims |
| 5 percent of teachers said their school gave victims resources to get fake images removed. | Teacher reports in the same survey | Same limits |
| In 2025 the Internet Watch Foundation (IWF) assessed 8,029 AI-made images and videos as realistic child sexual abuse. 3,443 of these were videos, up from 13 in 2024. 97 percent of the images showed girls. | Material the IWF assessed, not how much exists | IWF analysts only count material they can recognise as AI-made. Encrypted spaces are poorly visible. The count is not a measure of how common the abuse is |

What these figures do not show:

- **The share of all fake nude images that are of women.** We did not find a figure we could check. A widely repeated claim that 98 percent of deepfakes are pornographic comes from a vendor report whose data cannot be audited. We do not use it.
- **How many reports mean how many victims.** Hotline report counts, such as those from NCMEC in the United States, count reports. One image can be reported many times, and one report can hold many images. A letter from Stanford researchers says that 380,000 of the 485,000 AI-related reports NCMEC received in the first half of 2025 came from one company, and that at least 78 percent involved no AI-made abuse material. We do not use report counts to measure how common the abuse is.
- **Harm to adults versus children.** Studies of children and studies of adults use different methods.

## Why the harm is real

The picture is fake, but the person is real. A 2025 study of more than 16,000 adults in 10 countries (Umbach and colleagues, CHI 2025) asked about image-based sexual abuse in general, not only deepfakes. Of those who reported it, 61.4 percent reported at least one harm, and 33.4 percent reported a negative effect on mental health. That study does not separate deepfakes from other kinds of image-based abuse.

NCMEC, the US child protection charity, states that when exploitative images are made up, the harm to children and their families is still very real.

## What exists today

People can take steps now. The [help guide](get-help.md) lists them. They include StopNCII.org for adults, Take It Down for images of people under 18, reporting forms on each platform, and laws in a growing number of places. Each has limits, and the guide states them.

These routes act after an image exists. They do not stop a fake from being made.

## What Mantelet asks

Mantelet asks a narrow question: can we make it much harder to turn an ordinary photo of a woman or a child into a fake nude image, by adding small changes to the photo that people cannot see?

Earlier research suggests the answer may be hard. Several studies report that hidden changes to photos often stop working when someone attacks them on purpose, and that simple steps such as resizing can remove them. We list these studies in the [claims ledger](../research/claims-ledger.md) and [bibliography](../research/bibliography.md). We designed our tests so that we would find these failures if they exist, and we publish what we find, even a "no".

## Why a negative result still helps

A parent who trusts a tool that does not work may stop being careful. Showing, with public data, that a kind of protection fails is useful. It tells people where not to put their trust, and it sends effort toward routes that work today.
